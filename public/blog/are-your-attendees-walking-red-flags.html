Red Flags

Are your attendees walking red flags?

August 2026 · 5 min read

Somewhere on your attendee list is a person whose travel does not line up. On paper they look fine. In reality their flight, their room, and their transfer are telling three different stories, and they are heading straight for your event. That is a walking red flag, and you usually meet them at the worst possible moment: the front desk, the curb, the gate.

What a walking red flag looks like

They rarely announce themselves. A walking red flag is the attendee who registered and then never had a flight booked. It is the person with a confirmed hotel room and no record of ever registering. It is the executive whose plane lands the day after the room was held, or the guest routed to an airport an hour from the venue while everyone else flies into the one next door.

Each one looks harmless in its own spreadsheet. The registration list says they are coming. The flight manifest says they are flying. The rooming list says they have a bed. The problem is that no single list can see the others, so nobody notices that the check-in is December 4 and the room is booked for December 5. The misalignment only becomes real when the person does.

Why you do not see them coming

You are not missing these because you are careless. You are missing them because the information is scattered on purpose. Registration comes from your event tool, flights from a travel desk or the attendees themselves, rooms from the hotel, transfers from a ground vendor. Four sources, four formats, four different moments in time. Reading them side by side, your eye slides right past the gap, because a gap is an absence, and absence is the one thing scanning rows is worst at catching.

So the red flag stays invisible right up until it is standing in front of you. By then the fixes are expensive. The block has closed. The fare has jumped. The room is gone. What would have been a quick email in week two becomes an apology and a credit card in the lobby.

Catch them before they board

There is a better moment to meet a walking red flag, and it is early, on a screen, while the fix still costs nothing. That is what cross-checking does. Instead of reading each list on its own, you line them all up against each other and let the mismatches surface. Registration against flights. Flights against the hotel. Dates against dates. The few records that do not agree rise to the top, named and specific, and everyone who is fully aligned quietly clears.

The picture you want is the calm one. Three hundred green checks. Four red flags. Zero surprises. You are not hunting through tabs anymore. You are looking at a short list of the only people who need you, with the exact gap spelled out next to each name, weeks before any of them reach an airport.

Red should only ever mean a flag. When your list is mostly green, that is not luck, it is proof the event lines up. And when four names show red, you have the one thing a spreadsheet never gives you in time: the chance to fix them while they are still fixable.

Do not find the red flag at the gate

The travelers whose travel is not aligned are not a rare problem. In any group of a few hundred, a handful of them are on your list right now. The only question is whether you meet them in minutes, on a screen, or in person, on arrival day, when it is too late to do anything but pay.

Run the check early and the choice makes itself.
