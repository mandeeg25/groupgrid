Field Guide

Coordinating international travel for executive roundtables

August 2026 · 6 min read

An executive roundtable is a small event with an outsized margin for error. Twenty or thirty of the most senior people in an organization — and their counterparts, their board members, their most important clients — converging on one city from a dozen others. The headcount is tiny. The number of ways a single arrival can go wrong is not. Coordinating international travel at this level isn’t logistics with the volume turned down; it’s logistics with the tolerance turned down to zero.

Why cross-border VIP travel is a different problem

For a large domestic program, a mistake is a rounding error — one person in five hundred lands at the wrong terminal and the plan absorbs it. For a roundtable, that one person is a keynote, a chairman, or the reason half the room agreed to come. There is no crowd to hide the miss in, and no second flight that afternoon to quietly rebook onto. The stakes and the standards both run high, and they run high for every single name.

The scale of the underlying spend is a reminder of how much rides on getting this right. GBTA projects global business travel spending to reach $1.57 trillion in 2025, and international travel is the segment most exposed to disruption — the part of the portfolio where a delayed visa or a missed connection does the most damage. A roundtable concentrates exactly that exposure into a handful of high-value trips.

The paperwork that grounds a trip before it starts

Two documents decide whether an executive travels at all, and both fail silently if nobody checks them early.

Passports and visas

A passport that expires inside six months, a visa that hasn’t come through, an executive who assumed their old multiple-entry stamp still covered the market — any one of these ends the trip before the car is booked. And the runway to fix it has gotten longer. The U.S. Travel Association reports that first-time visitor-visa applicants from the top international source markets face interview wait times that have run to hundreds of days in some countries. If an attendee from an affected market needs a visa, that conversation cannot start the month before; it has to start the moment the invitation goes out.

Name-on-ticket matching

The name on the ticket has to match the travel document exactly — that is the whole point of the TSA’s Secure Flight program, and equivalent rules apply at borders worldwide. It sounds trivial until you remember who you’re booking for. Executives carry middle names, suffixes, hyphenated or maiden names, and passports issued in a formal legal name nobody uses day to day. A ticket booked as “Jim Reyes” against a passport reading “James A. Reyes-Okafor” is a problem you want to find in the office, not at the gate.

Arrivals scattered across every time zone

A domestic event has an arrival day. A global roundtable has an arrival window that can stretch across two calendar days and every time zone in between. One guest lands at dawn off a red-eye; another arrives near midnight; a third crosses the international date line and shows up, by the local calendar, before they left. Each of those arrivals implies a different check-in time, a different transfer, and a different tolerance for delay — and the flight file and the hotel file almost never speak the same language about when “arrival” actually is. A flight landing at 11:40 p.m. against a hotel whose standard check-in closed hours earlier is a stranded VIP, and the mismatch is invisible until you line the two files up side by side.

Private jets, commercial cabins, and everything between

Roundtable travel rarely runs on one kind of aircraft. Some principals fly private, some fly commercial in a premium cabin, some share a chartered leg, and a few change their minds a week out. That mix fragments your data: private movements arrive as an FBO schedule with tail numbers and a general-aviation terminal, commercial legs arrive as a GDS or agency manifest, and the two formats have almost nothing in common. A private arrival doesn’t clear the main customs hall or roll up to the same curb, so a transfer booked off the commercial arrivals board will wait at the wrong building entirely. Whoever owns the trip has to hold both pictures in their head at once — or, more reliably, get them into one place where they can be compared.

Ground transfers, one market at a time

There is no single car vendor for a program that touches six countries. You’re contracting a different chauffeur company in each market, each with its own confirmation format, its own idea of what a “meet and greet” includes, and its own local knowledge of which airport and which terminal. Reconciling those transfers against the real arrivals is a per-market job:

  • Wrong airport. Large cities have several. A car dispatched to the wrong one is a VIP standing alone at the curb of an airport with no ride.
  • Wrong terminal. International versus domestic, commercial versus the FBO for private arrivals — same airport, very different pickup point.
  • Time-zone drift on the transfer. A pickup time keyed off the departure clock instead of the local arrival clock, and the car shows up hours off.
  • Booked-but-nobody-arriving. A car still on the schedule for a guest who changed flights, or cancelled, and never made it back onto the vendor’s sheet.

The assistant problem

Here is the wrinkle that turns a hard job into a genuinely fragmented one: senior executives don’t book their own travel, and they don’t all book it through you. An executive assistant arranges the flight independently, the hotel gets reserved through the event block, and the car is handled by a local office — three owners, three files, no shared view. Your registration list says the executive is coming; it has no idea what the assistant booked. The only way to know whether those decisions actually add up is to lay the registration list next to the flight, hotel, and transfer files and check that every name lines up across all of them.

Reconcile it before arrival day, not at the curb

Every failure above shares one trait: it is invisible in any single file and obvious the moment two files are compared. A registered executive with no flight on record. A flight with no hotel to match it. A car meeting a plane that landed at a different terminal, or a different airport, or not at all. This is a reconciliation problem, and reconciliation is precisely what reading each spreadsheet on its own can never catch — the gap only shows up in the space between the files.

Doing that comparison by hand, across formats and time zones and a mix of aviation types, is slow and easy to get wrong under deadline. It’s worth building the habit anyway: cross-check the attendee list against the flight, hotel, and transfer data as a standing step, and treat every mismatch as a flag to clear before anyone boards. Whether you run that check in a shared sheet or in a tool built for it, the discipline is the same — and for a roundtable, it’s the difference between a smooth arrival and an incident involving someone you very much did not want to strand.
